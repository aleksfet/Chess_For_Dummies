What Is Chess and How Does It Work?

Chess is a two-player strategy game played on a 64-square board. White moves first, and each side starts with 16 pieces that each move in their own way. The main goal is to checkmate the opponent's king, meaning the king is under attack and has no safe escape. Games can also end by resignation, timeout, stalemate, or draw.

What makes chess fun is the strategy behind every move. Players create plans, attack and defend, set traps, win material, promote pawns, and use tactics like forks, pins, and checkmates to outthink their opponent. You can play chess on a physical board with friends or in tournaments, and you can also play online through websites and apps.

Chess History & Terms

Chess History

Chess has ancient roots, growing out of early strategy games played many centuries ago. Early forms of chess developed and changed over centuries, and the modern game we know today gradually became popular around the world. In time, chess became a serious competitive game, organized through clubs, tournaments, and world champions. More recently, online chess — websites, apps, and streaming — made the game even more accessible, and the COVID-era online boom introduced millions of new players to chess.

For a deeper history, view Chess.com's History of Chess article.

Key Chess Terms

Checkmate The king is under attack with no legal escape. This ends and wins the game.
Stalemate A player has no legal moves but isn't in check. The game ends in a draw.
Blunder A bad move that loses material or the game. Spotting yours helps you improve.
Fork One piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at once, so they can't all be saved.
Pin A piece can't move because doing so would expose a more valuable piece behind it.
Promotion A pawn reaching the far side turns into a stronger piece — usually a queen.
Elo A number estimating a player's strength. It rises with wins and falls with losses.
OTB "Over the board" — playing chess in person on a real board, not online.
En Passant A special pawn capture where a pawn can capture another pawn that just moved two squares forward, as if it had only moved one square.
Battery When two or more pieces line up to attack together, often with one piece supporting or protecting another piece's attack.
Threefold Repetition A draw rule where the same position appears three times during a game, with the same player to move.
50-Move Rule A draw rule where either player can claim a draw if 50 moves pass without a pawn move or a capture.

Openings, Endgames, and Improvement

Chess is not just about memorizing moves. It is a deep strategy game built on understanding patterns, plans, and positions. Players improve by learning openings, studying endgames, solving tactics, playing practice games, reviewing mistakes, and building better study habits over time.

Openings Shape the Game

Openings are the first moves of a chess game. They help you control the center, develop your pieces, and keep your king safe, while choosing the kind of position you want to play. As a beginner, learn the simple opening principles first — there's no need to memorize long lines yet.

Endgames Teach You How to Finish

The endgame is the phase when only a few pieces remain. Studying king activity, pawn endings, rook endings, promotion, and basic checkmates teaches you how to turn a small edge into a win and finish winning positions cleanly.

Tactics and Study Habits

Tactics and puzzles build pattern recognition, training your eye to spot ideas like forks, pins, skewers, discovered attacks, and checkmates. Good study habits make this stick: solve puzzles consistently, read chess books, watch instructional videos, and learn from stronger players.

Practice Games, Online Elo, and Review

Playing online games on Chess.com or Lichess helps you gain real experience, and your online Elo rating gives a rough way to track your progress. Reviewing and analyzing your own games helps you understand your mistakes and improve. Online chess offers different formats and is fun, user-friendly, and easy to practice with.

Legends, Time Controls, and Variants

Learn about some of the greatest players in chess history, then explore the game speeds and fun variants that make chess even more exciting.

Widely considered the strongest player ever, Magnus reached the world's highest rating. He is famous for grinding out wins from quiet positions.

Time Controls

Chess can feel very different depending on how much time each player has. Online and over-the-board games use time controls to decide how fast the game is played.

Bullet
Very fast games, usually 1–2 minutes per player.
Blitz
Fast games, often around 3–5 minutes per player.
Rapid
Medium-speed games, often around 10–30 minutes per player.
Classical
Longer games, common in serious over-the-board tournaments.
Increment
Extra seconds added after each move, like the "+2" in a 3+2 game.
Delay
A short grace period before your clock starts counting down on each move.

Chess Variants

Besides standard chess, there are many other fun variants that change the rules, starting position, or team format.

Chess960 / Fischer Random
The back-rank pieces start in randomized positions, so players rely less on memorized openings.
Bughouse
A team variant where captured pieces can be passed to your partner and placed on their board.
Crazyhouse
Captured pieces can be dropped back onto the board as your own pieces.
Puzzle Rush / Puzzle Storm
Fast puzzle modes where players solve tactics under time pressure.

OTB Tournaments and USCF

OTB means "over the board" — chess played in person on a real, physical board instead of online. In OTB tournaments, players usually use chess clocks, follow pairings that decide who plays whom, write down their moves using notation, and compete in sections grouped by skill level, all while earning official ratings. In the United States, the USCF (US Chess Federation) is the main organization that runs rated tournament chess.

The Elo Rating System and International Chess

Elo ratings are numbers that estimate how strong a player is, going up when you win and down when you lose. Online ratings, USCF ratings, and FIDE ratings all use similar ideas, but they are related rather than exactly the same, so the same player can have different numbers on each. FIDE is the international chess federation, and it is connected to international ratings, official titles like Grandmaster, and global chess competition.
